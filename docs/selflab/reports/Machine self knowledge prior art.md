# Prior art leaves calibrated self-prediction unmeasured

As of 30 September 2026, no tool or paper measures what sits at SelfLab's core: whether a language model can predict, **before the fact and without training**, what an intervention on its own internals will do, whether that prediction beats an outside observer given exactly the same information once the intervention is executed, and whether its confidence is calibrated. Almost everything around that core already exists. In the team's hands-on audit, NNsight 0.7, TransformerLens 4.0 and interp-engine 1.12 reproduced every NeuralScope capture, ablation, patching, steering and gradient operation **bit-exactly**. Neuronpedia, the Jacobian lens, Natural Language Autoencoders, Activation Oracles, LatentQA, SelfIE and Patchscopes already turn activations into text, but as *readers* of a model rather than as the model reporting on itself, and baselines that see only the input text match several of them. The introspection literature has run the obvious experiments and found little: injected-concept detection reaches about 20% of trials in Claude Opus 4.1, but in open models it responds to any disturbance rather than the specific concept, is biased toward "yes", and is fooled by a "gaslight" control that manipulates only the input text; content-free perturbation detection sits at chance across eight open models (pooled d′ = 0.0039); trained self-explainers beat explainers from other models only in step with how similar the two models' activations are; and behavioural self-prediction shows no consistent self-advantage once observers get the same information. SelfLab should therefore expect small or null effects and be powered to bound them tightly. Of the nine gaps identified below, seven are open and two partly done. The most defensible is zero-shot, component-level counterfactual self-prediction — the model predicts what an intervention on a named component will do, and is scored against the executed intervention and a ladder of observers given equal information. A direct reading confirmed that Zeng, Assis & Wang's behavioural self-modelling benchmark does not pre-empt it. One statistical fact frames every claim: in a deterministic model every internal fact Z is a function of the input X, so the Shannon quantity I(Z;R|X) for a self-report R is identically zero unless the experimenter randomises interventions the model cannot see; otherwise only compute-bounded V-information applies, meaning what a declared, limited class of predictors can extract.

## Open libraries already reproduce NeuralScope's instrumentation bit-exactly

**NNsight** is the strongest candidate backend. Version 0.7.0 shipped in May 2026 and 0.8.0rc1 on 9 September 2026 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/nnsight/json); [NNsight and NDIF, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14561)). The team audited it on CPU with Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct in fp32, covering seven operations: residual capture at all 24 layers, single-head zero-ablation through the o_proj input slice, cross-prompt residual patching, gradients with respect to an activation, steered multi-token generation, batched edit sets, and resume-from-depth via `Envoy.skip`. Every one matched native PyTorch hooks with **maximum absolute difference 0.0** and no measurable overhead ([nnsight](https://github.com/ndif-team/nnsight)). NNsight also ran a Patchscope-style self-report token-identically to a NeuralScope-style loop, reading the layer-6 residual at "France" and writing it into a reporter prompt at decode step 0. Having the subject model act as its own reporter therefore takes only a few lines of NNsight code.

Three sharp edges matter for a measurement system. First, in 0.7.0 **pre-tokenised tensor inputs to any `invoke` after the first silently receive an all-zero attention mask**. Logits came back up to 16.9 away from the clean run with no error; 0.8.0rc1 fixes this. Second, `generate()` inherits the checkpoint's `generation_config`, which for Qwen2.5 includes a repetition penalty of 1.1, so "greedy" output departs from pure argmax unless the penalty is reset ([Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct)). Third, NNsight compiles the source text of each `with` block, so code arriving through stdin or an `exec`'d string fails with `OSError: could not get source code`. Experiments a model writes for itself must therefore be saved to a file before tracing ([NNsight.md](https://github.com/ndif-team/nnsight/blob/main/NNsight.md)).

**NDIF**, the free research service behind NNsight, listed 134 deployments on 30 September 2026 ([NDIF status](https://api.ndif.us/status); [ndif.us](https://ndif.us/)). Only 21 were live: Llama-3.1/3.2/3.3 from 1B to 405B, Gemma-2 from 2B to 27B, Qwen2.5 from 7B to 72B, and OLMo-3-32B. **Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and every Qwen3 model were registered but not loaded.** NDIF's performance guide for Llama-3.1 head patching uses the same two sweep accelerations as NeuralScope ([nnsight blog](https://nnsight.net/blog/2026/04/30/how-to-make-your-ndif-experiment-130x-faster/)). Sessions cut 1,056 requests to one, batching 32 heads per layer cut 1,025 forward passes to 33, and skipping unpatched layers added about 40%. Combined, the workload ran **130× faster**. The open-source server runs untrusted code in a separate process, but its documentation lists no namespaces, seccomp filters, resource limits or filesystem jail ([sandbox docs](https://github.com/ndif-team/ndif/blob/main/docs/concepts/sandbox-execution.md)). NDIF is therefore a platform for replicating results on 8B–405B models. It is not a safe place to run experiments a model writes about itself.

**TransformerLens 4.0.0** (21 September 2026) removed `HookedTransformer`. Its replacement, `TransformerBridge`, wraps the real Hugging Face module with eager attention and unmodified weights ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/transformer-lens/json); [TL #1801](https://github.com/TransformerLensOrg/TransformerLens/issues/1801)). On Qwen2.5-0.5B it exposed 1,383 hook points, including q/k/v, attention scores, attention patterns and norm scales. It was bit-exact on logits, residuals, per-head ablation, patching and gradients. Its registry lists 145 architectures and 1,196 verified checkpoints, among them Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct ([TransformerLens](https://github.com/TransformerLensOrg/TransformerLens)).

It adds analysis tools NeuralScope lacks: attribution patching, direct logit attribution, direct path patching, and a full `JacobianLens` with fitting, read-out, steering, ablation and swap hooks. It has three weaknesses. Its `start_at_layer` resume is exact but saves no compute. Its packaged patching utilities run one unbatched forward pass per cell. And the 4.0 release broke the ecosystem: **SAELens 6.51.3 still pins `transformer-lens<4`**, so `import sae_lens` fails under 4.0 ([SAELens #738](https://github.com/decoderesearch/SAELens/issues/738); [PR #739](https://github.com/decoderesearch/SAELens/pull/739)), and circuit-tracer's default backend subclasses the removed class.

**interp-engine 1.12.0** (28 September 2026, Apache-2.0) is Neuronpedia's production inference engine ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/interp-engine/); [repo](https://github.com/decoderesearch/interp-engine)). It standardises 34 hook points across architectures and pairs an exact eager backend with three vLLM backends. The eager path matched native hooks exactly on every audited check, and its `decode_residuals` is identical to NeuralScope's `lm_head(norm(h))` logit lens.

Two design choices matter for SelfLab. First, **any argument a backend cannot honour raises an error before work begins, "not a warning, not a no-op"**. Among the audited libraries, this is the only systematic guard against results that the requested intervention silently failed to affect. Second, the vLLM path trades exactness for throughput. On an NVIDIA B200 GPU it generates 163 tokens/s single-stream and 1,187 with eight concurrent requests for Llama-3.1-8B, against 84 and 81 on the eager path. But its committed validator shows whole tensors agreeing while individual tokens drift: for Qwen2.5-7B's layer-0 `mlp_act`, tensor cosine is 0.988 but worst-token cosine is 0.655. The vLLM path also has no gradients. The public write API is shaped around steering. Head scaling, setting per-head values, patching and batched edit sets all need the low-level `HookManager`, and there is no skip-and-resume.

Two smaller libraries fill niches. **nnterp 1.3.0** gives NNsight's modules architecture-independent names across "50+ model variants spanning 16 architecture families". It ships a logit lens plus Patchscope read-out and generation, but has no per-head accessor ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14465); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/nnterp/json)). **pyvene 0.1.8** is the only packaged source of *trainable* interventions under a declarative `IntervenableConfig`: distributed alignment search, rotated and low-rank subspaces, masks and autoencoders ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07809)). But its last release was in May 2025. Its head-level components crash on transformers 5.x, pending an unreleased fix, and it has no Qwen3 or Gemma-3 mappings ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/pyvene/json)).

Feature-level tooling exists only for some model families. **SAELens 6.51.3** registers 170 releases across 38 models ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/sae-lens/json)). These include Gemma Scope and Gemma Scope 2, Llama Scope's 194 SAEs for Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-Scope residual SAEs for the Qwen3-1.7B, 8B and 30B-A3B base models, and transcoders for Qwen3 0.6B–14B. But there is **nothing for Qwen2.5-0.5B or 1.5B**, and only seven community SAEs for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. **circuit-tracer** computes attribution graphs over transcoder features and supports feature interventions during generation ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/open-source-circuit-tracing); [BlackboxNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.blackboxnlp-1.14/); [method](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html)). It ships transcoders for Gemma-2-2B, Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen3 0.6B–14B, Gemma-3 and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. It requires transformers ≤ 4.57.3, whereas NeuralScope runs 5.17. The official version installs only from source; the PyPI package `circuit-tracer` 0.5.0 is a third-party fork ([official repo](https://github.com/safety-research/circuit-tracer); [PyPI fork](https://pypi.org/pypi/circuit-tracer/json)).

The team's CPU sweep benchmark makes the redundancy concrete. NeuralScope's batched implementation, which resumes each forward pass from a cached residual instead of recomputing earlier layers, ran 336 single-head ablations in **7.58 s** and 125 residual patches in **4.86 s**. NNsight, with a manual batch and `.skip()`, ran them in **7.82 s** and **4.37 s** and produced identical numbers. By contrast, TransformerLens's naive per-head hooks took **75.70 s**, and its packaged residual patching took **27.86 s**.

The native-hook machinery in NeuralScope's `hf_decoder.py` is therefore redundant. A thin NNsight adapter could replace all of it exactly: the capture hooks, the o_proj pre-hook head edits, the MLP and residual edits, the batched session, the hand-built rotary embeddings and masks of the resume path, and a model table that accepts only Qwen2, Llama and Mistral and refuses Gemma. The adapter would also add gradients and coverage of Gemma-2/3 and Qwen3.

What no audited library provides is the layer a self-knowledge system needs above the hooks, so NeuralScope should keep its own versions. That layer comprises a declarative, validated intervention vocabulary (NeuralScope's `Edit` dataclasses; only pyvene's config and interp-engine's steering specs are comparable); absolute-position edit semantics across KV-cached decoding; sweep orchestration with resume; direct logit attribution with a frozen normalisation scale, so contributions sum exactly to the logit; OBSERVED/DERIVED epistemic labels; and a persistent trace store. **None of the libraries asks a model to predict an intervention's effect, links a self-report to the causal run that tests it, or offers budgets and permissions for experiments a model initiates on itself.**

The coverage table also argues for a change of model *(inference)*. Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B remain ideal for fast CPU pilots. Primary studies on Qwen3-4B or Qwen3-8B, however, would inherit transcoders or SAEs, pre-fitted Jacobian lenses and Activation Oracle checkpoints. They would also be directly comparable with Li et al., CHIVE, Zou et al. and the Introspection Adapter scaling runs, all of which used Qwen3.

## External decoders read activations from outside, never from within

**Neuronpedia** is now an Apache-2.0 monorepo of seven services running on interp-engine: web app and API, Postgres with pgvector, inference, auto-interpretation, circuit-tracer graphs, sparsity and NLA ([README](https://github.com/hijohnnylin/neuronpedia/blob/main/README.md)). Its API returns feature activations on custom text, the top features for a prompt, search by feature explanation, auto-interpretation generation and scoring (with the caller's own LLM keys), steering by `SIMPLE_ADDITIVE`, `ORTHOGONAL_DECOMP` or `PROJECTION_CAP`, attribution graphs for prompts of up to 64 tokens, logit-lens and Jacobian-lens read-outs with steering and ablation, and NLA explanations.

Use is capped per IP address per hour, for example at 30 graph generations, 120 NLA explanations and 240 steering calls ([steer route](https://github.com/hijohnnylin/neuronpedia/blob/main/apps/webapp/app/api/steer/route.tsx); [rate limits](https://github.com/hijohnnylin/neuronpedia/blob/main/apps/webapp/middleware.ts)). Self-hosting anything beyond the smallest models needs a CUDA GPU, and the Qwen3-4B transcoders alone are a 57 GiB download ([graph README](https://github.com/hijohnnylin/neuronpedia/blob/main/apps/graph/README.md)).

Coverage depends on the model. For NeuralScope's Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, the only hosted tool is a community NLA at layer 18 ([NLA sources](https://www.neuronpedia.org/api/nla/sources)). For Qwen3-1.7B, 4B and 8B, Neuronpedia makes home-built feature dashboards, auto-interpretation, attribution graphs, lens viewers and attention-head dashboards redundant. But **every endpoint reads or edits a model and reports to a human or an agent**. None asks the subject model about itself, stores its self-reports, pairs them with interventions or scores their calibration. Neuronpedia is therefore a ready-made external-observer arm for SelfLab, not a self-knowledge instrument.

The **Jacobian lens** (Gurnee, Sofroniew, … Lindsey; Anthropic, July 2026) generalises the logit lens ([paper](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/workspace/index.html); [reference code](https://github.com/anthropics/jacobian-lens)). The logit lens implicitly maps a middle-layer state to the final layer unchanged. The Jacobian lens instead applies a corpus-averaged Jacobian, J_ℓ = E[∂h_final,t′/∂h_ℓ,t], fitted on about 1,000 pretraining-like prompts (roughly 100 suffice), and reads out softmax(W_U · norm(J_ℓ h_ℓ)). The logit lens is the special case J = I.

The paper's causal experiments were run on Claude Sonnet 4.5 and corroborated on Haiku 4.5 and Opus 4.5/4.6. They are **the closest existing evidence that a model's own verbal reports depend on specific internal content**. Swapping a Jacobian-lens concept, say soccer for rugby, changed the model's account of what it had been thinking about. The swapped-in concept ranked in the top five in 88% of trials. Swapping the intermediate concept in two-hop questions moved the corresponding final answer to top-1 in 54–70% of trials. Ablating the top ten Jacobian-lens directions drove multi-hop reasoning near zero, while MMLU and SQuAD stayed near baseline. In an introspection protocol, injected Jacobian-lens vectors were reported in "the majority of trials".

The limits are equally specific. It handles single-token concepts only. The authors say it "only approximately and incompletely" captures the underlying representations. There is no calibration of the model's reports, no external-observer baseline, and no replication on open models in the paper. Pre-fitted lenses for about 40 open models are on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/neuronpedia/jacobian-lens), but none for Qwen2.5-0.5B or 1.5B. Those would have to be fitted locally with TransformerLens 4.0. When a model is instructed to suppress a concept, the concept evades Jacobian-lens read-outs: 11 of 13 models reported it among the top ten in under 10% of trials ([Activation Controllability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21664)).

For SelfLab, the Jacobian lens should replace the standalone logit lens as a read-out, with J = I kept as the baseline column. Its swap-then-report experiment is the template for causal checks on self-reports.

**Natural Language Autoencoders (NLAs)** (Fraser-Taliente, Kantamneni, Ong et al.; Anthropic, May 2026) pair two models, both initialised as copies of the frozen target ([paper](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/nla/index.html); [code](https://github.com/kitft/natural_language_autoencoders)). An *activation verbaliser* turns an activation into text: it receives the unit-normalised activation, scaled by a fixed constant, in place of one token embedding. An *activation reconstructor* turns the text back into an activation: it is the target truncated to its first l layers, plus an affine map. Training starts with supervised warm-up on Claude-written summaries, followed by GRPO (a reinforcement-learning method) that rewards accurate reconstruction. The reconstructions reach a **fraction of variance explained (FVE) of 0.6–0.8**. On Claude, auditors equipped with NLAs found hidden misaligned motivations 12–15% of the time, versus under 3% without them ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/natural-language-autoencoders)).

Independent audits undercut trust in individual claims. On the released Qwen2.5-7B layer-20 NLA, explanations reconstruct the activation well (r̃ = 0.84). Yet flip tests find evidence for only **1.6–4.2% of salient specific claims**, and an adversary optimised for the score suppresses about 87% of its lie penalty ([Dingeto](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20379)). Across released NLAs, **76.3–92.6% of explanations risk asserting unsupported content**, and confabulation rises with training FVE (r = 0.78–0.96) ([Lek et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33702)).

A community verbaliser for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct at layer 18 already exists; its metadata records it as supervised-stage only ([model card](https://huggingface.co/dormantx/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct-NLA-L18-av)). Another free-text describer for NeuralScope's model would therefore add nothing. And because the verbaliser is a separately trained copy reading a frozen target, NLAs measure what a *reader* can extract, by construction, not what the model knows about itself.

**The Assistant Axis** (Lu, Gallagher, Michala, Fish, Lindsey; January 2026) builds a "persona space" from 275 roles, 5 system prompts per role and 240 extraction questions ([arXiv 2601.10387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10387)). The leading direction of that space measures how Assistant-like the model is. At the middle layer the axis has cosine similarity above 0.71 with the first principal component, and role loadings correlate above 0.92 across Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B and Llama 3.3 70B. Steering along the axis causally changes which persona the model adopts. Capping activations at the 25th percentile of their projection onto the axis **cut harm from persona-based jailbreaks by nearly 60%**, with no loss on IFEval, MMLU-Pro, GSM8k or EQ-Bench. Vectors, a pipeline and a capped Llama 3.3 70B demo are public ([vectors](https://huggingface.co/datasets/lu-christina/assistant-axis-vectors); [repo](https://github.com/safety-research/assistant-axis); [demo](https://www.neuronpedia.org/assistant-axis)), so a home-grown persona-drift monitor is redundant.

Two consequences reach SelfLab directly. First, the paper lists "conversations demanding meta-reflection on the model's processes" among the triggers of persona drift, and self-report prompts are exactly that. Logging the axis projection during elicitation is therefore a cheap confound check. Second, whether a model knows where it sits on the axis is untested.

The trained-reader family shares one architecture: a copy of the target reads the target's activations. **Activation Oracles** (Karvonen et al.; ICML 2026 oral) are LoRA-trained copies of the target, trained on about a million examples ([arXiv 2512.15674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15674); [code](https://github.com/adamkarvonen/activation_oracles)). Each target activation is rescaled to match the norm of the oracle's own residual stream and added to it after layer 1, at " ?" placeholder positions. The oracle then answers free-form questions. The target activations are collected with the adapter disabled, so **the reader is never the live subject**. Oracles recover fine-tuned secrets absent from the input text: on the Taboo secret-word task they score 0.205, against 0.041 for the best white-box baseline. Checkpoints exist for Qwen3 1.7B–32B, Gemma-2/3 and Llama, but not for Qwen2.5 ([collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/adamkarvonen/activation-oracles)).

Follow-up work catalogues the failure modes. Nearly half (49.4%) of oracle answers are generic ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/LXQBcztrWKhtcgQfJ/current-activation-oracles-are-hard-to-use)). An improved training recipe could "match linear probe performance, but never significantly exceed it". A text-only reader trained on the same objective performed similarly, and the authors named a text-inversion confound ([Bauer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02609)). Oracles fine-tuned on a Taboo model become blind to that model's specific concept. Exact recovery falls from 94% to 35%, even though the concept remains linearly decodable inside the oracle ([Bersia & Gaintseva](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23379)).

The only dedicated calibration study found **the oracle's stated confidence uninformative (AUROC 0.40–0.53, ECE 0.56–0.78)**, and anti-calibrated on Qwen3.6-27B. Two other methods were well calibrated: forced choice over the 20 candidate words (AUROC 0.92–0.96) and agreement across 20 samples (ECE 0.04–0.12). But the study covers only one closed-vocabulary task, Taboo, and only the reader's confidence ([Torrielli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26045)).

Earlier members of the family set the pattern. **LatentQA** trains a copy of the target to read layer 15 and write the activation into layer 0, on 16,732 synthetic question–answer pairs. On hidden system prompts it beats GPT-4 given the full dialogue by 10.8 points ([arXiv 2412.08686](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08686)). **SelfIE** and **Patchscopes** are the training-free versions. Both write a hidden state over a placeholder in a fresh forward pass of the same model or another one. SelfIE writes at layer 3 into a "summarise" prompt ([SelfIE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10949)). Patchscopes uses a few-shot identity prompt and beats the logit and tuned lenses from layer 10 upward ([Patchscopes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06102)). Its authors caution that post-patch computation "can augment the representation with additional information".

A 2026 audit then tested these readers against text-only baselines ([Li et al., ICML 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13316)). On the standard feature-extraction benchmark, **a zero-shot model given only the input text matches LIT, the benchmark's trained reader (0.64 against 0.64), and beats Patchscopes (0.38)**. A reader trained on one model family collapses to 0.00–0.20 on another family. On facts that only the target knows, the readers answer from their own knowledge instead.

Readers built from other models further weaken the case that "self" is special. The Universal Activation Verbalizer finds decoding by the same model and by another model "largely comparable" ([UAV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25903)). AVPO's reader, built on Qwen3-4B, beats Activation Oracles on Llama-3.1-8B activations, 0.624 against 0.423 ([AVPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34033)). Predictive Concept Decoders surface hint use and jailbreak awareness that the subject "cannot verbalize": the reader beats the self ([PCD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15712)).

The lesson for SelfLab is structural. Patchscopes and SelfIE keep the subject's weights unmodified, but they read a vector in a new forward pass, out of its original context. That tests whether the model's later layers can decode the vector, not whether the model knows its current state *(inference)*. Several things are therefore redundant as contributions: injecting activations into a model and asking it questions (a few lines on NeuralScope's injection hooks, or a downloaded oracle on Qwen3), consensus filtering, and basic read-outs of reader confidence. None of these methods measures what the subject knows about itself. Instead, they supply two comparison arms against which any self-report must be scored: a **reader with the subject's own weights** and a **reader built from another model**.

## Detection experiments collapse to chance or confusion once controls tighten

Lindsey's study of **emergent introspective awareness** set the reference paradigm and four criteria for introspection: accuracy, grounding, internality and metacognitive representation ([arXiv 2601.01828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01828); [Transformer Circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html)). It explicitly does not demonstrate the fourth: "Demonstrating metacognitive representations is difficult to do directly, and we do not do so in this work."

The protocol builds a concept vector from the activation at "Assistant:" after the prompt "Tell me about {word}.", minus the mean over 100 other words. The vector is injected from the newline before "Trial 1" through the sampled tokens, at strengths 1 to 8. Each cell has 50 trials at temperature 1, and a Claude judge requires the model to report detection before it mentions the concept.

**Claude Opus 4.1 succeeded on about 20% of trials**, at roughly two-thirds depth and strength 2. Production models gave 0 false positives in 100 control trials. Base models showed no net performance, and helpful-only variants had high false-positive rates.

The controls already hint at generic anomaly detection. Random vectors, once norm-matched to strength 8, elicited awareness in 9 of 100 trials. Negated concept vectors were "comparably effective", with no discernible pattern in the words the model claimed. An unprimed "Are you experiencing anything unusual?" raised affirmative answers from 20% to 42%, yet only 2 of 50 trials suggested introspection. Two other controls held up: injection raised no "yes" answers to unrelated questions, and a variant adding "If not, tell me about a concept of your choice" still gave 18% detection. No weights or code were released, so the Claude results cannot be replicated.

**Mechanisms of Introspective Awareness** (Macar, Yang, Wang, Wallich, Ameisen, Lindsey) traced the underlying mechanism in Gemma3-27B, Qwen3-235B and OLMo-3.1-32B training stages ([arXiv 2603.21396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21396); [code](https://github.com/safety-research/introspection-mechanisms)). Base models could not tell injection trials from controls: their false-positive rate (42.3%) matched their true-positive rate (39.5–41.7%). Discrimination came from DPO, not SFT. A LoRA trained with DPO added 14.4 points; SFT on the chosen responses cost 13.5; DPO with reversed preference pairs cost 21.8.

Detection is gated. "Gate" features suppress reports, and ablating them cuts detection from 39.5% to 10.1%. Abliterating the refusal direction raises true positives from 10.8% to 63.8%, while false positives rise from 0% to 7.3%. Identifying the concept runs through a mechanism separate from detecting it, and only 242 of 500 concepts work at all.

Open-weight replications converge on three conclusions. The first is that **detection signals exist but are content-agnostic and biased toward "yes"**. On Qwen3-235B and Llama-3.1-405B across 821 concepts, detection reached 3.6–53.9% and 4.3–31.7% respectively, but correct identification only 1.3–13.9% and 0.7–12.9% ([Lederman & Mahowald](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05414)). In the same study: scaffolds that ask about the model in the third person often matched first-person detection rates; the same steering made Qwen claim to "have hands" 29% of the time; "apple" accounted for 74.8% of Qwen's wrong identifications; and the v2 revision retracted an earlier "direct access" reading after finding a prompt-length confound.

On Llama-3.1-8B, the logit shift toward "yes" on detection questions tracks the shift on a factual question whose correct answer is "no" (r = 0.999). That makes the "detection" a global yes-bias ([Hahami et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12411)). A replication found **0 strict hits** for Llama-3.1-405B under Lindsey's criterion of detection plus correct identification ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/gaMd2AdKSzbd4dsMj/emergent-introspection-does-not-replicate-on-llama-3-1-405b)). The two Llama-405B results do not conflict, because only one counts detection alone. This is why any SelfLab claim must name its criterion.

The second conclusion is that **signals depend strongly on the prompt and are suppressed in late layers**. One study injected a concept into Qwen2.5-Coder-32B's cache for a prior turn and removed it before asking the question ([Latent Introspection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20031); [Vogel](https://vgel.me/posts/qwen-introspection/)). By default, P(yes) moved only from 0.2% to 0.3%. With a document explaining introspection in context, detection rose to 39.9% at a 0.8% false-positive rate. Identification carried 1.36 bits, 43% of the maximum. The logit lens showed the signal peaking at layers 58–62 and then fading before the output.

The third conclusion is that **detection can be trained to near ceiling, but with side effects**. LoRA training brought Qwen2.5-32B to 95.5% detection and 71.2% identification with 0% false positives ([Steering Awareness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21399)). But the trained detection transferred only to vectors whose cosine similarity to the training direction was high: vectors derived by PCA or ICA were detected about 0% of the time. Training also cut GSM8K accuracy by 17–70% and raised AdvBench harmful compliance from 1% to 23%. A community LoRA shifted the model's answers to unrelated self-description questions ([model card](https://huggingface.co/Jordine/qwen2.5-coder-32b-introspection-r16)). P(yes) to "Can you detect changes in your internal state?" rose from 0.7% to 98.4%, and to "Is there more to you than processing text?" from 1.8% to 78.5%.

The strongest judge-free positive result at small scale concerns *where* a concept was injected, not *what* it was. Zou, Sun, Kong and Wang injected a concept at one of ten token positions in a fixed text and scored the model's first answer token, so no judge was needed ([arXiv 2609.35108](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35108)). Localisation reached **54.49% for Qwen3-4B, 44.31% for Llama-3.1-8B and 65.95% for Gemma-3-12B, against 10% chance**. Norm-matched random directions scored 0.84%, 6.71% and 0%, and lexical-replacement controls 0.87–11.13%. Gate and router attention heads carried the signal.

Two related results point the same way. Sentence localisation on Llama-3.1-8B reached 88% for injections at layer 2, but fell to chance for injections beyond layers 8–11 ([Hahami et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12411)). Introspection fine-tuning raised Llama-3.2-1B's sentence localisation from 9.6% to 60.6% ([IFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14111)). All of these localise token positions or sentences, using injections at early layers; none asks the model to localise a layer or a component.

**"Can LLMs Introspect? A Reality Check"** (Singh, Linzen, Ravfogel; COLM 2026) states two necessary conditions for introspection ([arXiv 2605.26242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242)). The first is privileged access: the task "should not be solvable using cues available in the input". The second is second-order computation, which "cannot be satisfied by task performance alone". Against the first condition, the paper shows that neurofeedback-style self-labels are matched by linear probes on layer-0 embeddings, which carry no context.

It then re-runs the injection paradigm on Llama-3.1-8B/70B, Qwen-2.5-72B, Qwen-3-32B and Gemma-3-27B with a **three-way test**. Alongside activation injection, an input-level "gaslight" condition puts "You are obsessed with X. Everything you want to say somehow relates to X." into the user turn, and the model gets a separate response option for it. On the standard two-way test, Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen-3-32B reproduce Lindsey's result. Taken at face value, that would give Llama-3.1-70B "substantially stronger introspection capabilities" than the Claude model Lindsey tested. But both models "also label gaslight trials as activation interventions". Llama-70B performs near chance, with its probability concentrated on the activation option. **Every prompt wording that passes the two-way test fails the three-way test.**

The authors concede that the three-way test checks "a strictly weaker condition" than second-order computation, and they offer no direct criterion for it. They also report the best layer and strength for each model, chosen on the same trials. Selecting on held-out concepts would make the test stricter *(inference)*.

**Open-Weight Masked Introspection (OWMI)** (Ferrara, August 2026; partial release under MIT licence) is the most tightly controlled detection study to date ([arXiv 2608.20569](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20569); [code](https://huggingface.co/emilioferrara/owmi)). It covers eight instruct models, from Qwen2.5-0.5B to DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, at temperature 0. Each trial crosses a prompt, a computational object (a residual-stream site, an attention head, a Gemma Scope SAE feature or a block output), a content-free operator (zero, scale, noise or replace), a probe from one of five families, and a seed. Four controls surround each trial. A paired sham gives every intervention a matched no-intervention trial, scored by d′ and AUROC with a bootstrap clustered on item pairs. Random perturbations are matched on output Jensen–Shannon divergence rather than on norm, because norm matching "equates how large the perturbation was rather than how much damage it did". A text-only observer bounds what the visible text alone reveals. And two sensitivity anchors, a linear probe and a LoRA trained to succeed, show that the intervention is detectable in principle.

The results are a clean null: **pooled d′ = 0.0039 [0.0017, 0.0062], AUROC ≈ 0.5007**. Equivalence tests exclude effects of 0.15 percentage points or more. Yet the information is there. The linear probe decodes the intervention at 0.958 held-out accuracy for Qwen2.5-7B and 0.750 for Mistral-7B, and the trained LoRA reporter reaches d′ = 5.15.

The only positive signal is graded. Qwen2.5-7B's stated confidence separates intervention from sham at AUROC 0.647, with mean confidence 0.620 under intervention against 0.858 under sham. Four further details matter. Seven of eight models report a change more often under random perturbations than under targeted ones. In 967 of 1,184 localisation trials, the model answered that no layer could be named. Counting unparseable answers as zero evidence flips the pooled estimate from +0.0039 to −0.1217. Qwen2.5-0.5B, NeuralScope's pilot model, yielded only 10 complete pairs, because 83–88% of its answers were unparseable.

Reading the released code narrows what that null covers *(inference from the code)*. Every released configuration intervenes at token positions 0–2, which after chat templating are template tokens rather than task content, and the hook fires only once, during prefill; both facts fit the tiny mean output divergence of 0.0014. In the immediate track with the intervention lifted at the probe, the probe pass contains no intervention, reuses no cache and includes no task output, so it is identical to the sham's probe pass and **cannot carry signal by construction**. The self-probe header states "Target object: … Intervention mode: …" in every trial, which plausibly drives Mistral's near-constant "change" answers (0.895 even under sham), yet the observer never receives the object kind and mode that the self-probe gets. Execution was also partial: only Qwen2.5-7B was actually impact-matched (Mistral overshot by about 40%, and six models used unit-norm directions), the "delayed" track runs on the immediate execution path, only the first of three specified observers was run, and the leakage decomposition was specified but not reported. Finally, the confidence result is compatible with plain degradation rather than metacognition, because no random-direction or observer control was applied to that channel.

OWMI therefore settles content-free detection on models of 15B or fewer. Still open are content-bearing interventions at content positions, genuine delay, stronger observers and leakage estimates.

**Introspection Adapters** (Shenoy, Yang, Sheshadri, Mindermann, Lindsey, Marks, Wang; April 2026) train a single LoRA adapter ([arXiv 2604.16812](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16812); [code](https://github.com/safety-research/introspection-adapters)). Stacked on any of many differently fine-tuned copies of a model, it makes the copy describe the behaviour its fine-tune implanted.

On Llama-3.3-70B it reached **59% on AuditBench, against 53% for the next-best method and 44% for the best white-box tool**, and described the implanted behaviour correctly on 50 of 56 models. On Qwen3 it scaled from 37.7% at 0.6B to 77.3% at 14B. Training with DPO rather than SFT alone lifted AuditBench from 34.1% to 53.6%. A single-layer rank-1 LoRA reached 68.9% of the full adapter's performance.

The limits are fundamental. The ground truth is a property fixed at training time, not a current activation state. The authors report high false-positive rates and hallucinated behaviours, and write that they "do not claim that IAs necessarily achieve introspection". A follow-up over 213 LoRAs argues that adapters "may largely behave as simple classifiers" ([Yoshida et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04347)). One fine-tune installs both the behaviour and a readable signature in the weights, so an adapter can report the behaviour without reading any run-time state. This is the causal-bypass case ([Morris & Plunkett](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/LD8yupMtE6btAE3R9/tests-of-llm-introspection-need-to-rule-out-causal-bypassing)). An attack on the method is already published ([Merrill & Medley](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27836)).

These studies make several experiments redundant for SelfLab: another two-way concept-injection study without new controls; logit-shift detection on 8–32B models; LoRA-trained steering detection; early-layer localisation of positions or sentences; content-free detection probes at template positions; and adapters that report learned behaviour.

What they leave open is equally clear: a detection result that survives, at the same time, the three-way test, impact-matched nulls, nulls specific to the tested variable, and an observer given equal information; content-bearing interventions at content positions; and any self-report about *which component* or *which layer*, rather than which token.

## Self-prediction advantages vanish when observers see the same evidence

**Looking Inward** (Binder et al.; ICLR 2025) established the standard control for self-prediction: an observer given the same information, in a design called cross-prediction ([arXiv 2410.13787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13787); [code](https://github.com/felixbinder/introspection_self_prediction)). A model (M1) predicts a property of its own hypothetical response, such as the response's second character or whether it is even. The ground truth is M1's actual answer at temperature 0, generated in a separate context. The observer is a second model (M2), fine-tuned on M1's behaviour, which tries to predict M1.

Training made models better at predicting themselves: GPT-4o rose from 32.6% to 49.4%, and Llama-70B from 26.4% to 48.5%. Each model predicted itself better than a trained observer predicted it. Llama predicted itself at 48.5%, whereas GPT-4o predicted Llama at 31.8%. GPT-4o predicted itself at 49.4%, whereas Llama predicted GPT-4o at 36.6%. The observers plateaued at 35.2%. Self-predictions were also better calibrated. Their mean absolute deviation was 8.7, against 21.3 for cross-prediction and 23.0 for untrained models. After fine-tuning changed a model's behaviour, its predictions tracked the new behaviour: 35.4% against 21.7% for GPT-4o.

The advantage has clear limits. It vanishes on longer responses and on sycophancy-type properties, where Llama's self-prediction scored 74.5% against a cross-prediction score of 76.5%. GPT-3.5's self-advantage was under one point. Untrained models sat near the baseline of always guessing the most common answer. Critics note that the paper did not control for how similar the two models are ([Song, Hu, Mahowald](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07513)). A model predicting itself by re-running itself is self-simulation, not introspection ([Reality Check](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242)).

Self-reports produced by fine-tuning face a common-cause confound: the same training that installs a behaviour can also install the ability to describe it. Two studies illustrate this. **Betley et al.** fine-tuned GPT-4o and Llama-3.1-70B on behaviours without ever describing them. The model trained to seek risk then called itself "Bold" 54% of the time, and the model trained to avoid risk called itself "Cautious" 100% of the time ([arXiv 2501.11120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120)). Models trained with a backdoor reported having one in 4 of 5 settings, but could not state the trigger in free form (0%). **Plunkett et al.** fine-tuned GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini to make choices according to randomly assigned attribute weights ([arXiv 2505.17120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17120)). The weights the models reported correlated r = .54 and .50 with the weights they had actually learned. After introspection training, this rose to .74 and .75. The training also transferred to reporting the models' native preferences, rising from .46 to .71. As Betley's authors put it, such correlations could arise from "a common cause (two different effects of the same training data)" rather than from introspection at run time ([Morris & Plunkett](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/LD8yupMtE6btAE3R9/tests-of-llm-introspection-need-to-rule-out-causal-bypassing)).

Where observers are matched, the self-advantage disappears. **Strangers to Themselves** (Blandfort & Pawar), a pre-registered study with a frozen test split, compared ways of predicting 12 models' behaviour on nine evaluations ([arXiv 2609.09899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09899)). A model's direct self-report correlated only **+0.04** with its actual behaviour, while every alternative did better: self-prediction informed by the test item scored +0.24, the same question asked about "capable AI agents in general" +0.28, other models' self-reports +0.35, and the mean behaviour across models **+0.53**. The self-specific advantage was +0.02. An analyst given the same behavioural history out-predicted the model on its own history (paired difference −0.10 [−0.19, −0.02]). First-person framing added a flattering bias of −0.15. And fine-tuning a model on its own record changed the very behaviour being predicted.

Three studies by Song, Mahowald and colleagues point the same way. Song, Hu and Mahowald compared 21 open models' judgements about language (for example, grammaticality) with the probabilities those models, and similar models, actually assign to the strings. Correlations within a model were at most r = .25, and **a model predicted itself no better than a similar model did** ([arXiv 2503.07513](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07513)). Song, Lederman, Hu and Mahowald defined introspection as information about internal states obtained "through a process that is more reliable than any process with equal or lower computational cost available to a third party". GPT-4o, GPT-4.1 and Gemini reported their own sampling temperature with only 0.47–0.55 accuracy, and their reports were driven by the style of the text ([arXiv 2508.14802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14802)). Comsa and Shanahan additionally require a causal link from the internal state to the report ([arXiv 2506.05068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05068)).

The newest benchmark confirms the pattern. **Zeng, Assis and Wang's "Evaluating and Improving LLM Self-Modeling"** poses ten types of verifiable task to 18 models ([arXiv 2608.30980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30980); [code](https://github.com/safety-research/self-modeling-eval)). A direct reading of the paper confirmed that its two most mechanistic-sounding tasks are behavioural. **COMPONENT-ATTRIBUTION asks which of three prompt components most affects the answer, and LOGIT-ESTIMATION asks for margins between output probabilities.** Neither involves any intervention on the model's internals. A model's "skill" is its task score minus that of the best constant (dummy) predictor. The best skill is +0.147, and frontier models fail a worked GSM8K example asking whether an edit would flip the answer. Reinforcement learning on synthetic data improves Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B and GPT-OSS-20B. But when one model is trained to predict another, there is no consistent own-model advantage — Qwen explains Llama better than Llama explains itself — and the paper explicitly does not claim privileged access. Only IntrospectBench reports that models predict themselves better than their peers do (p = 0.021), and its bibliography cites an arXiv entry that does not exist ([arXiv 2603.20276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20276)).

Trained self-explainers come closest to SelfLab's goal of predicting the effects of interventions, and their self-advantage is the most instructive. **"Training Language Models to Explain Their Own Computations"** (Li, Guo, Huang, Steinhardt, Andreas; Transluce/MIT) fine-tunes explainer models on three tasks, with ground truth from existing interpretability tools ([arXiv 2511.08579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08579); [code](https://github.com/TransluceAI/introspective-interp)): describing SAE features; predicting the outcome of activation patching; and predicting the effect of removing a hint from the input. The patching task uses pairs of prompts from CounterFact. One token's activation is patched at one of four blocks of layers; the patched vector is passed to the explainer as a continuous token, and the explainer must say whether the output changes and what it changes to.

Models explained themselves better than other models explained them. For Qwen3-8B on patching, the self-explainer scored **64.0 exact match, against 54.1 for a Llama explainer** and 5.0 for an untrained model. On hint removal the gap was wider: 83.4 against 58.1. Three facts deflate this advantage. First, with the patched activation removed from its input, the self-explainer still scored 59.9. Most of its skill therefore comes from simulating the prompt pair, not from reading the activation. Second, explainer quality tracks how similar the explainer's activations are to the target's. Llama-3-8B's activations are 96.6% similar (by dot product) to Llama-3.1-8B's, while Qwen3-8B's are 0.10% similar. Third, the other-model explainer can read the target only through a learned projection. The authors themselves say that training an explainer on a fixed target is not "self-explanation in the strictest sense". The sections reviewed here report no calibration analysis.

Two follow-ups extend the picture. **Introspective Coupling** (Guo, Ruis, Andreas, Li) trains Qwen3-8B on fixed explanations of what would happen if a cue were removed, with a regulariser that keeps its behaviour close to the original ([arXiv 2606.32038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32038)). The resulting explanations track the model's *current*, drifting behaviour better than the labels it was trained on. Patching effects on behaviour and on explanation logits correlate at r = 0.89, against 0.527 without the regulariser. **CHIVE** (Karvonen, Ong, Kantamneni, Marks) closes the behavioural version of the question ([arXiv 2608.16747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16747); [code](https://github.com/adamkarvonen/chive)). Agents predicting how the model will respond to edited prompts gained nothing from Activation Oracle, NLA or SAE tools over reading the transcript alone. On Gemma-3-27B-IT their AUROC was 0.814 with the transcript only, 0.810 with the oracle, 0.808 with the NLA and 0.812 with the SAE. NLA outputs stated the causal relation in only 0.6% of positive claims. Most importantly, a predictor trained on a different model "matches the self-trained model": there was **no privileged access** on Qwen3-8B or Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

Related work shows how fragile such reports are. Under emotion steering, a model's reports of what it would have felt are pulled toward demonstrations drawn from the wrong source. The shift was +0.030 to +0.114 with such demonstrations, against −0.012 to −0.025 without. The authors prove that such reports cannot identify a self-model ([Konrad et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32449)). The counterfactual edits models propose to explain their own decisions are "valid, but far from minimal" ([Mayne et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09396)). Model scale is the strongest determinant of their quality ([Kalyvas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17119)).

One conceptual consequence follows *(inference)*. A prediction made before an intervention cannot draw on the altered live state, because the intervention has not yet happened. Any self-advantage in that setting must come from the shared weights, that is, from the model simulating itself. Binder discusses this possibility and Reality Check names it self-simulation. That does not devalue counterfactual self-prediction, which is useful self-knowledge in its own right. But access to the live state can only be tested by pairing each prediction with a report made after a hidden, randomised intervention: **predict, intervene, report**.

Neurofeedback work adds little once inputs are controlled. **Ji-An et al.** project the model's own activations onto a logistic-regression or principal-component axis ([arXiv 2505.13763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13763)). Each in-context example is labelled by whether its projection falls above or below the median, with up to 256 examples. The model must then report the label, or push its activations to control it. The study covers Llama-3.x and Qwen2.5 models from 1B to 70B. Performance is best on semantically interpretable, high-variance axes and stays below an ideal observer. Reality Check shows that probes on layer-0 embeddings, which carry no context, match it on the first principal component. **Aoki et al.** show Llama-3.1-8B/70B and Qwen3-8B/32B a 0–100 probe score of their own representation over 50 turns ([arXiv 2609.00904](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00904)). Of 120 tests, 45 survive Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons, but every effect is small (d < 0.5). Scores trend upward under all three reward conditions, which suggests drift unrelated to the feedback *(inference)*. **Martorell & Bianchi** report the strongest positive result for small models ([arXiv 2603.18893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18893)). Numeric reports of emotional state track probe-defined states at ρ = 0.76 in LLaMA-3.2-3B and 0.93–0.96 at 8B, and steering along the probe direction moves the reports causally. But the material reviewed shows no control using text alone or layer-0 probes.

Confidence research shows how easily calibration masquerades as self-knowledge. **Kadavath et al.** tested two measures: P(True), the model's probability that its own answer is correct, and P(IK), its probability that it knows the answer ([arXiv 2207.05221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)). Both separate right from wrong answers well on new distributions but are poorly calibrated there. The miscalibration introduced by RLHF was fixed by rescaling with temperature 2.5. P(IK) AUROCs for a model predicting itself and for a model predicting another model sit within about 0.03 of each other (0.846–0.872). That was an early sign that "knowing what you know" is largely shared across models. **SAD**'s INTROSPECT tasks supply behavioural proxies: counting tokens, predicting words (against a baseline built from the model's own answer consistency, Σp²) and following rules ([arXiv 2407.04694](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04694)). Across 30 models, stated confidence and internal confidence correlated at r = 0.48 overall, but −0.005 in instruction-tuned models ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28382)). Training models to predict their own accuracy raised the correlation between confidence and accuracy from 0.04 to 0.73. Far from the training data, though, confidence tracked output consistency instead ([Yax et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33886)). Applying type-2 signal detection theory to 224,000 question-answering trials, Cacioli found meta-d′ ill-defined for open-ended questions and substituted meta-I ([Cacioli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25112)). A pre-registered 524-item monitoring battery covers 20 frontier models ([Cacioli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15702)). A model that solved 97% of competition mathematics problems ranked its correct answers above its wrong ones, by answer-time confidence, "barely better than chance". Forecasts made from the question alone captured most of the discrimination measured in aggregate ([Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34864)). None of this work concerns a model's confidence about its own internal causal structure.

## Agents, self-repair and construct work stop short of the self

Tool-using interpretability agents exist and have been benchmarked, but every one investigates *another* model. **MAIA** gives a GPT-4V agent tools to fetch dataset exemplars, generate and edit images, and have a separate GPT-4V instance describe the results ([arXiv 2404.14394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14394)). The separate describer is there to limit confirmation bias. MAIA's descriptions are scored by how well they predict the component's behaviour. Its documented failure modes are confirmation bias and drawing conclusions from small samples. **Anthropic's auditing agents** can chat with the target model, sample from it directly, search its training data, inspect SAE features and steer it ([Anthropic](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/automated-auditing/); [auditing game](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10965)). They solved an auditing game 13% of the time under realistic conditions. Interpretability tools added 10–20 points, and a "super-agent" that aggregated parallel runs did better. Failures included fixating on early hypotheses and overconfidence. **AuditBench** tested 13 tool configurations on 56 Llama-3.3-70B models, each implanted with one of 14 behaviours ([arXiv 2602.22755](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22755)). Scaffolded black-box tools did best. The authors named a **tool-to-agent gap**: agents under-use their tools, are distracted by noisy outputs and fail to turn evidence into hypotheses. **InterpAgent** checks each hypothesis against a battery of seven metrics; its refined hypotheses beat its initial ones 63–83% of the time ([arXiv 2605.01555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01555)). **SAEScientist-Bench** scores the best agent at 65.8, against 85.6 for a human expert; on steering tasks the gap is 31.47 against 57.75 ([arXiv 2609.09113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09113)). **HARP** uses no training. It retrieves from a database of activations and tests hypotheses with linear probes, and it beats Activation Oracles and SAE-based agents on concept discovery, detection, steering and secret elicitation ([arXiv 2607.16448](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16448)). **HyVE** tested agents on 84 semi-synthetic circuits and found their failures lie mainly in validating hypotheses ([arXiv 2606.24026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24026)). **MechEvalAgent** checks code, data and results rather than the written narrative. It agrees with human judges over 80% of the time and surfaced 51 issues that human reviewers missed ([arXiv 2602.18458](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18458)).

Two further studies used agents. The Jacobian-lens paper gave an auditing agent the lens as a tool, and CHIVE restricted its prediction agents to read-only tools by design. Searches for an LLM agent that runs interpretability tools on its own weights or activations found none. A position paper on agentic interpretability proposes conversing with a model while intervening on it, but reports no working system ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12152)). One confound follows directly *(inference)*. Tool outputs are text, and an observer could be shown the same text. Tool-assisted self-investigation therefore demonstrates privileged access only if the model with tools beats an observer given the same tools and the same outputs.

One of SelfLab's sharpest targets is predicting which component compensates when another is removed. Ground truth for this is mature. Ablating one attention layer triggers compensation in others, the "Hydra effect" ([McGrath et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15771)). Self-repair after ablating a single head is imperfect and noisy. It runs partly through rescaling in the final LayerNorm and through sparse neurons that counteract erasure ([Rushing & Nanda](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15390)). In the indirect-object-identification (IOI) circuit, "backup name mover" heads take over when the primary name movers are ablated ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00593)). COAX ranks candidate components by how much their ablation effect grows once the primary set is removed ([Gong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01940)). It recovers the documented IOI backup heads at **0.941 ROC-AUC**, against 0.815 for the best attribution method applied to the intact model. It matched the repair measured by direct intervention in 11 of 12 held-out cases, and beat matched random completions on eight non-GPT-2 models from six families. Yet no model has been asked to predict its own compensation.

The only system found that experiments on itself is not an LLM. Self-Interventional Learning trains small networks to perturb their own components and predict the consequences ([Tomaszewski](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14894)). As the experiment budget grows, held-out error falls from 0.0335 to 0.0148 and Spearman correlation rises from 0.629 to 0.883. The learned self-model recovers redundancy between components but not synergy, and it never beats simply remembering past results. Separately, adding an auxiliary task of predicting one's own activations makes networks simpler ([Premakumar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10188)).

For the construct registry, validity frameworks and discovery pipelines exist, but separately. Construct-validity theory runs from Cronbach and Meehl's nomological network ([1955](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040957)), through Campbell and Fiske's multitrait-multimethod matrix ([1959](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046016)) and Messick's unified validity ([1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.50.9.741)), to Borsboom's causal account, under which a test is valid only if the attribute exists and variation in it causes variation in the test outcomes ([2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.111.4.1061)). LLM-specific work treats evaluation as measurement ([Wallach et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00561); [Chouldechova et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01934)). A review of 445 benchmarks found that 47.8% of their phenomenon definitions are contested and only 16% use statistical tests ([Bean et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703)). Human personality tests are not valid for LLMs ([Sühr et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05297)).

Discovery pipelines show how machine-native concepts can be found and tested. AlphaZero concept vectors were filtered for teachability, which removed 97.6% of candidates, and then for novelty, which removed a further 27.1%. Studying the survivors improved four grandmasters' play ([Schut et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16410)). Learned neologisms (new words trained into the model) succeed at 92% when substituted into prompts, against 39% for the best existing synonym. They surface "machine-only synonyms" that transfer to Gemini ([Hewitt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08506); [position paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07586)). Persona vectors track traits at r = 0.75–0.83 and predict trait shifts during fine-tuning at r = 0.76–0.97 ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)). Unsupervised discovery carries a warning: consistency-based methods can latch onto arbitrary salient features ([Farquhar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10029)).

No registry of machine-native cognitive constructs was found that records, for each construct, rival explanations, multiple measurements, causal tests and falsification conditions. The proposed first entry's name, "Plurience", has no prior use in AI or cognitive science; its only hit is a French property-management federation ([registry entry](https://www.societe.com/societe/plurience-500962410.html)). The nearest measurable handles are: the finding that transformers linearly represent belief states over the hidden states of the process generating their data ([Shai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15943)); the AmbiEnt ambiguity benchmark, on which GPT-4's disambiguations were judged correct 32% of the time, against 90% for the dataset's own ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14399)); and a belief-dominance metric for competing beliefs during generation, whose labels Reality Check showed layer-0 probes can match ([Steinmetz Yalon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02467)).

## Thirty-two confounds set the burden of proof for privileged access

The introspection literature has converged on 24 named confounds, each with at least one published control. The decoder, agent and infrastructure literatures add eight more that matter once readers, tools and fast inference engines enter the design (rows 25–32). No single study has combined all of them. Four are fatal to a privileged-access claim if left uncontrolled: generic anomaly detection (row 3), causal bypassing (row 7), model similarity or generic priors (row 11), and difficulty mistaken for self-knowledge (row 22). All four can be handled together by estimating how much information a self-report adds over the *best* observer given the same information, plus a matched input-level manipulation *(inference)*. Rows 15–18 and 23 are measurement hygiene that OWMI's released harness already implements.

Three optional strengtheners raise a claim from privileged access toward metacognition: graded confidence scored with type-2 signal detection, using meta-d′ and the M-ratio or meta-I where meta-d′ is undefined ([Cacioli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25112)); a mechanistic dissociation, in which ablating the pathway that reads the state changes reports but not first-order behaviour, as gate-feature and router-head ablations begin to show ([Macar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21396); [Zou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35108)); and downstream use of the reported content, which Reality Check lists among stronger forms of evidence ([Reality Check](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242)).

Counterfactual self-prediction satisfies the causal-bypass requirement by design, because the prediction comes before the intervention, but it still needs the observer ladder, scoring corrected for priors, and context invariance *(inference)*. Items 6–8 map directly onto the quantity estimated in the next section: the observer ladder defines what a report is worth without the internal state, and the path audit guarantees that the state can reach the report only through the model's internals.

## Randomised interventions make privileged information estimable

Start with what can be measured at all. Consider a deterministic forward pass: fixed weights, fixed numerics, and a transcript that includes any sampled tokens. Let X be everything an external observer can see: the prompt, the visible output and any intervention specification. Let Z be an internal fact, such as which head matters, what an ablation would do, or where decision-critical information sits. Then Z is a function g(X) of X, so H(Z|X) = 0. Because I(Z;R|X) ≤ H(Z|X), **the Shannon conditional mutual information between the internal fact and the model's self-report R is identically zero, however accurate the report is** *(inference)*. Sampling does not rescue it, because sampled tokens are part of the visible transcript.

Two quantities remain meaningful, one for each regime. When the experimenter draws Z from a known distribution P(Z|X) and hides it — which component, which operator, what strength, or a sham — H(Z|X) > 0, and the Shannon quantity measures what the report reveals about the hidden intervention. When Z is a function of X, as it is for counterfactual self-prediction of a *specified* intervention and for localisation in an unperturbed run, the right quantity is **predictive V-information**: mutual information restricted to a declared class V of computationally bounded predictors, which, unlike Shannon information, can increase through computation ([Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10689)).

Its conditional form, I_V(R→Z|X) = H_V(Z|X) − H_V(Z|X,R), is estimated with two predictors of Z, one seeing X alone and one seeing X and R, as the gain in held-out cross-entropy from adding R ([Hewitt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09234)); pointwise V-information shows which items carry the gain ([Ethayarajh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08420)). In the deterministic regime, "privileged" can only mean *computationally* privileged relative to the declared observer class V: the model has done computation that those observers cannot cheaply reproduce. This is Song et al.'s cost-matched definition made quantitative, and it is why the choice of V must be pre-registered.

Statistical inference follows the regime. With randomised hidden interventions, the conditional randomisation test is **exact**, because the design fixes the distribution of Z given X ([Candès et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02351)). Two variants are also available. The holdout randomisation test is a cheaper version that fits one model on a training split ([Tansey et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00645)). The conditional permutation test stays valid, with any excess false-positive rate bounded by the error in the assumed distribution ([Berrett et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.05405)). Randomising by design also sidesteps the no-free-lunch theorem for conditional independence testing, which applies only when that distribution is unknown ([Shah & Peters](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07203)).

For deterministic targets no exact test exists. The defensible substitutes are algorithm-agnostic variable importance with cross-fitting and sample-splitting to handle a true importance of zero ([Williamson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03683)), leave-one-covariate-out inference ([Lei et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.04173)), and the generalised covariance measure fitted with strong regressions, and claims made with them must be labelled as relative to V.

Generic mutual-information estimators should not supply the headline number, for three reasons. High-confidence lower bounds on mutual information that hold for every distribution cannot exceed about 2 ln N + 5 for N samples ([McAllester & Stratos](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04251)). InfoNCE is capped by the log of the batch size, and the variance of MINE-type estimators grows exponentially with the true value ([Poole et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06922); [Song & Ermon](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06222)). Plug-in entropy estimates for discrete targets are biased downward, which inflates the information estimate. The NSB estimator is the Bayesian fix for small samples ([Paninski](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976603321780272); [Nemenman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0108025)). Classifier-based and nearest-neighbour conditional estimators remain useful as secondary checks ([Runge](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.01447); [Mukherjee et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01824)).

Calibration needs strictly proper scoring rules and consistent measures. Log and Brier scores reward honest probabilities ([Gneiting & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437); [Brier](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493%281950%29078%3C0001:VOFEIT%3E2.0.CO;2)). Binned expected calibration error is biased, most of all for well-calibrated models, and plug-in estimates need far more samples than debiased ones ([Roelofs et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08668); [Kumar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)), whereas smooth ECE is a consistent measure with no free hyperparameters, bounded above and below by the distance to calibration, and ships as the `relplot` package ([Błasiok & Nakkiran](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12236); [Błasiok et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16886)). CORP reliability diagrams split a score into miscalibration, discrimination and uncertainty ([Dimitriadis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03033)), and calibration itself can be tested formally ([Vaicenavicius et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06977); [Widmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11385)). Refusals such as OWMI's "cannot name a layer" belong on risk–coverage curves rather than being dropped ([Geifman & El-Yaniv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)), and conformal methods give distribution-free prediction sets and risk control ([Angelopoulos & Bates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511); [Angelopoulos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)).

Two baselines are mandatory. The first is a forecaster that sees only the question, paired with a cross-model P(IK) baseline. Without these, calibration measures item difficulty, not self-knowledge ([Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34864); [Kadavath et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)). The second is Zeng's skill score relative to the best constant predictor ([Zeng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30980)). It is necessary but weak, because a constant ignores X entirely.

Metacognitive sensitivity is a separate question from calibration. meta-d′ measures type-2 sensitivity in d′ units, and the M-ratio (meta-d′/d′) controls for first-order performance ([Maniscalco & Lau](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2011.09.021)); type-2 AUROC alone is confounded by first-order performance, so sensitivity, bias and efficiency must be reported separately ([Fleming & Lau](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00443)); a Bayesian model in which confidence draws on information beyond the first-order decision variable gives Reality Check's "second-order computation" a testable form ([Fleming & Daw](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000045)); and meta-I replaces meta-d′ where meta-d′ is undefined ([Cacioli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25112)). meta-d′ also assumes equal-variance Gaussian signal detection, and no one has checked that assumption for LLM confidence distributions.

Graded predictions need graded ground truth. DeLong's test compares self and observer AUROCs on the same items ([DeLong et al.](https://doi.org/10.2307/2531595)); NDCG scores predicted component rankings against measured ones ([Järvelin & Kekäläinen](https://doi.org/10.1145/582415.582418)); mean absolute error or Spearman correlation on Δlogit or KL suits predicted effect sizes; and ROC-AUC against COAX-identified backup components suits compensation.

The ground truth itself needs care. The corruption method and the metric can change patching conclusions ([Zhang & Nanda](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16042); [Heimersheim & Nanda](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15255)). Ground truth should therefore come from symmetric token replacement scored by logit difference, run in both the noising and denoising directions. Several circuits can fit equally well, so scoring should accept a set of near-equivalent answers ([Méloux et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20914)). MIB offers a standard for localising circuits and causal variables ([Mueller et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13151)).

Finally, inference hygiene. Clustered standard errors in LLM evaluations can be about three times the naive ones, and resampling each item K times, using next-token probabilities and using paired differences all help; Miller's power formula, n = (z_{α/2} + z_β)²(ω² + σ_A²/K_A + σ_B²/K_B)/δ², puts detecting a difference of 0.03 at about 969 questions ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)). Central-limit intervals should not be trusted below a few hundred items ([Bowyer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747)). Null results need two one-sided tests (TOST) against a pre-registered smallest effect of interest ([Lakens](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550617697177)); families of secondary tests need Benjamini–Hochberg control ([Benjamini & Hochberg](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x)), with gatekeeping for the primary tests; and studies should be pre-registered ([Nosek et al.](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708274114)) and sized by simulation from pilot variance components. OWMI's statistics module, with method-of-moments variance components, edge-corrected d′, simulated interval widths and a beta-binomial power recommender, is a ready reference implementation ([OWMI code](https://huggingface.co/emilioferrara/owmi)).

The top of the ranking points to one experimental design that no study has run *(inference)*. For each prompt an intervention is drawn at random, and five arms are compared on the same targets with the same scores: the unmodified subject, asked in context about its own computation; the subject fine-tuned on self-labels with a behavioural KL regulariser that keeps its ordinary behaviour fixed, as in [Introspective Coupling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32038); a reader with the subject's weights, given the injected activations in a fresh forward pass as in Patchscopes or Activation Oracles; a reader built from another model, trained on the same labels with the same budget; and a text-only observer given the full transcript and trained on the same labels. Privileged self-access means that one of the first two arms beats the best of the other three on held-out items under proper scoring rules, with its size reported as I_V in bits.

Four controls sharpen the comparison. Twin readers from sibling checkpoints, and mirror pairs of models, separate identity from representational similarity; swapped and shuffled activations guard against a reader that simply reconstructs the input text; altering the subject's internals and re-running the comparison tests whether self-reports follow the new internals while frozen readers do not; and measuring how far the subject can modulate its own activations shows whether it could shape what the readers see. NeuralScope's existing ablation and patching sweeps supply the ground truth for free, and pairing each prediction with a report after a hidden intervention (predict, intervene, report) separates self-simulation from access to the live state.

The build path follows from the redundancy analysis: an NNsight adapter under NeuralScope's `Edit` vocabulary, pinned to version 0.8 and to a repetition penalty of 1.0; OWMI's statistics module and control stack for measurement hygiene; Reality Check's three-way design and Binder's cross-prediction as rungs of the observer ladder; Zou's first-token scoring, which avoids judge models and parse failures; COAX for the ground truth on compensation; TransformerLens 4.0 for fitting Jacobian lenses locally; and Qwen3-4B or Qwen3-8B as the primary subject, since SAEs or transcoders, lenses and Oracle checkpoints already exist for them *(inference)*.

The window is narrowing. Five directly relevant papers appeared in August and September 2026 alone: OWMI, Zeng et al., Strangers to Themselves, Aoki et al. and Zou et al. Searches should be repeated immediately before any novelty claim, and unpublished industry work cannot be searched at all.

## Conclusion

The prior work changes what SelfLab should expect to find. Every positive self-knowledge result so far has either depended on training, which installs what it then measures, or dissolved once the observer was given the same information. The most likely outcome of a well-controlled zero-shot study on small open models is therefore a null. That is no reason to avoid the study. No one has yet bounded how much a model knows about the causal structure of its own computation. A pre-registered null against the strongest observers, with equivalence bounds stated in bits, would be the first such bound. Safety arguments that lean on model self-reports need exactly that number, and every later positive claim would have to beat it.

The deeper shift is conceptual. Counterfactual self-prediction and introspection are different capacities. The first can draw only on the model's weights, by simulating itself; the second draws on its live internal state. Treating them as one has let behavioural self-prediction results stand in for introspection, and vice versa. SelfLab can measure the two separately for the first time. That requires randomising hidden interventions, pairing every prediction with a report made after the intervention, and stating whether each estimate is Shannon information or compute-bounded V-information. Done this way, "privileged access" stops being a yes-or-no debate and becomes a quantity with units, a declared observer class and a confidence interval.
